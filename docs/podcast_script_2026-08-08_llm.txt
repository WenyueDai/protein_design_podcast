The paper doesn’t say whether any of those tools consistently hit sub‑angstrom RMSD for the binding pocket, but if we could measure that, it would tell us which architecture is worth fine‑tuning for metabolite sensing. A natural next step would be to run a blind benchmark on a set of nanobodies with known crystal structures bound to diverse small molecules — say, twenty targets spanning sugars, phosphates and hydrophobic drugs — and record not just global RMSD but also the precision of side‑chain rotamers in the complementarity determining regions. That would surface failure modes like over‑rigid CDR loops or misplaced aromatic cages that the current metrics might miss.

The biosafety preprint raises a different kind of question: if sequence‑similarity screens are obsolete for models that can invent entirely new folds, what structural or physicochemical signatures could we monitor instead? One idea is to track the distribution of predicted solvent‑accessible surface area or the prevalence of buried charged residues in generated sequences, since extreme values often correlate with toxicity or membrane disruption. We could start by collecting a small library of de novo designs from a language model, compute those features with a fast tool like DSSP, and see whether any simple thresholds flag the known harmful examples from the literature. That would give us a concrete failure mode to guard against before we even think about experimental testing.

Turning to Hsc70, the cross‑linking mass spec data gave us a 71 kDa view of the chaperone under physiological and stress conditions like hypoxia, heat shock and pH shifts. I’m curious how the inter‑domain distances change when the nucleotide state flips from ADP‑bound to ATP‑bound. If we could replicate those cross‑linking experiments in a defined nucleotide cocktail and then model the resulting distance constraints with RosettaCM, we might catch subtle hinge motions that static crystal structures hide. Comparing the predicted flexibility to the experimental B‑factors from the same samples would be a direct test of whether our models capture the allosteric ripple that drives substrate release.

The antibody optimization paper hints that all‑atom interaction models plus pretrained language models could finally move us beyond trial‑and‑error maturation. What would be a meaningful metric to track as we iterate? Perhaps the change in calculated binding free energy per round of mutagenesis, correlated with the actual ELISA EC50 shift observed after expression. If we could show that a 0.5 kcal/mol improvement in the computational score reliably translates to at least a two‑fold gain in potency across a panel of ten diverse antigens, we’d have a quantitative feedback loop worth automating. Running that loop on a publicly available antibody‑antigen benchmark like SAbDab would let us see whether the approach generalizes beyond the specific virus they studied.

Finally, the gallstone risk model used 319 individuals — 157 patients, 162 controls — with 38 features ranging from bioimpedance to routine labs. I wonder how the SHAP values shift when we add a simple dietary variable like daily fat intake, or when we stratify by age decade. If we could obtain a larger cohort — say, a thousand subjects from a biobank — and retrain the Bayesian‑optimized SVM, logistic regression and decision tree stack, we could check whether the same features stay top‑ranked or whether new ones emerge, especially in the younger subgroup where lifestyle factors might dominate. Tracking the calibration curve across those sub‑populations would tell us whether the model remains trustworthy when deployed in a community clinic rather than a research setting.

All of these threads feel like they could start with a modest computational experiment — re‑scoring a published set, adding one extra feature, or probing a specific nucleotide state — and then loop back to the bench for validation. That back‑and‑forth is where the next generation of protein design tools will likely be forged.
