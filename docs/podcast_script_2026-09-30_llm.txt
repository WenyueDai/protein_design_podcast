The biggest shift today is that secondary structure information, long treated as a mere intermediate step, is now being fed directly into all-atom diffusion models as a first-class input. A paper on nucleic acid structure prediction called OFoldNA shows that when you give a diffusion model base-pairing maps alongside the sequence, accuracy jumps — and the variant OFoldNA-SS2TS, which uses accurate base-pairing information, improves both folding and co-folding precision. This matters because secondary structure is far easier to obtain than 3D coordinates, so you're no longer bottlenecked on sparse structural data. The same auxiliary branch doubles as OFoldNA-SS for RNA secondary structure prediction, where it achieved the best out-of-distribution performance on the CHANRG benchmark. For someone designing protein-nucleic acid complexes, this means you can now leverage readily available pairing information to guide co-folding of longer DNA chains and protein-DNA interfaces, which is exactly where traditional methods have struggled.

The second major shift is that tokenization, not just parameter count, is becoming the lever for efficiency in protein language models. A paper called LEMON-ZEST introduces evolution-informed tokenization, or ZEST, which derives its vocabulary from conserved regions of multiple sequence alignments. The result is a 200M-parameter model called LEMON that outperforms state-of-the-art models ranging from 600M to 3 billion parameters on remote homology detection. The compression is dramatic — four residues per token on average, so 4,000 residues fit within a standard 1,024-token context window. Training completed on a single H100 GPU in one week. This flips the scaling narrative: instead of throwing more compute at bigger models, you can embed biological priors at the tokenization stage and get better results with a fraction of the resources.

The third insight is a cautionary one about D-peptide design. A paper introducing Mirror-Score tested raw ProteinMPNN negative log-likelihood as an affinity ranker across 31 crystal structures from four target families. The pooled Spearman correlation was only 0.19, and worse, the correlation reversed between the MDM2/CHIP family at plus 0.62 and the gp41 family at minus 0.70. For the viral-entry family, interface pLDDT from Boltz-2 mirror-space cofolding achieved a leave-one-out Spearman rho of 0.90, correctly ordering all three peptides by affinity while NLL failed at 0.18. This tells D-peptide designers that sequence-compatibility ranking alone is unreliable, and that family-matched calibration is the practical deployment mode.

The fourth shift concerns the amino acid alphabet itself. A survey of over 250 million sequences in UniProt revealed that proteins composed of reduced amino acid alphabets are extremely rare in nature, suggesting that the full 20-amino-acid repertoire is not arbitrary but functionally constrained. This has direct implications for anyone trying to minimize the design space in de novo protein construction.

The fifth and final shift is about data infrastructure. A guide called EXPERT proposes a structured framework for capturing protein expression and purification metadata, recognizing that inconsistent experimental reporting remains a major barrier to building effective machine learning models in protein engineering. The paper doesn't specify the exact template fields, but the core message is clear: without standardized metadata, even the best models will be limited by the quality and consistency of the data they're trained on.

What strikes me about these papers is how each one leans on a different kind of shortcut, and the question is whether those shortcuts are principled or just convenient. Take OFoldNA-SS2TS — the clever move is using secondary structure as a conditioning input rather than trying to predict it implicitly. A naive diffusion model would have to learn base-pairing topology from scratch, which is exactly where nucleic acid folding falls apart on longer chains. The control that makes this convincing is the ablation: when they strip out the SS branch, accuracy drops, and when they feed in accurate base-pairing, it climbs back up. That's the kind of orthogonal validation I'd steal — I'd want to know whether my model is actually using the SS signal or just ignoring it. But here's what's fragile: the paper doesn't say how they generated the partial SS inputs for the partial-information experiments. If those came from a different predictor than what you'd use in practice, the gains could evaporate. An expert would notice that the OOD win on CHANRG is impressive, but CHANRG is an RNA secondary structure benchmark — it doesn't test whether the SS conditioning transfers to DNA or to protein-nucleic acid co-folding, which is where the real bottleneck is.

The LEMON-ZEST paper is even more interesting because it's essentially asking: can you replace compute with biology? The naive approach would be to scale up a standard transformer on raw sequences, but that hits the context window wall — 4,000 residues won't fit in 1,024 tokens without aggressive downsampling. ZEST solves this by compressing four residues per token, which means you're not just saving memory, you're changing what the model sees. But what's fragile is the assumption that conserved regions in MSAs are the right thing to tokenize. If your target family has few homologs, the MSA is sparse, and ZEST's vocabulary collapses. An expert would notice that the paper trained on a single H100 for a week — that's a strong claim about efficiency, but it also means the training data was probably curated to fit that constraint. You could easily imagine a scenario where ZEST works great on Pfam but falls apart on metagenomic sequences with no clear conservation signal.
