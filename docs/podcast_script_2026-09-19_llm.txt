The most non‑obvious insight today is that a simple confidence metric — pLDDT — from a structure‑prediction model can serve as a quantitative proxy for binding affinity in linear epitope mapping, bypassing the need for energetic calculations.  

The most elegant idea is the use of a temporally partitioned protein‑language‑model benchmark (SCL2205) to expose and correct hidden training‑testing leakage, turning a data‑curation problem into a principled way to measure true generalisation in protein deep learning.

Thinking about the AlphaFold2 epitope paper, the way they turned a peptide‑scanning window into a pLDDT‑ranked predictor makes me wonder how the confidence scores would behave if we swapped the glycine‑placeholder MSA for one that retains the actual physicochemical diversity of the antigen loop — would the correlation with ELISA binding stay above 0.8 for longer, more flexible peptides, or does the drop they saw with version 3 stem from the MSA construction itself? The paper noted optimal performance with AlphaFold2‑multimer v2 and MMSEQS 2022, yet v3/MMSEQS 2302 gave reduced accuracy; could a hybrid approach that caches the v2 MSA but runs the v3 model recover the lost precision without sacrificing speed?  

Moving to the chromatin condensate piece, the abstract gave almost no methodological detail, which leaves a gap I’d like to fill: what coarse‑grained force field did they use to reproduce the phase diagram of nucleosome‑rich environments, and how did they validate the material properties — did they compare simulated shear moduli to FRAP recovery rates measured in live cells? Knowing those numbers would let us ask whether the same model can predict how a point mutation in a low‑complexity domain shifts the saturation concentration by, say, 0.5 mM, a shift that could be tested with turbidity assays.  

The SCL2205 motivation paper flags metric inflation from unmitigated training‑to‑testing overlap; a concrete follow‑up would be to construct a hold‑out set where no protein shares more than 30 % sequence identity with any training example, then report both the traditional accuracy and the newer calibrated‑expected‑calibration‑error metric — does the gap shrink from 12 % to under 4 % when the overlap is removed?  

For VirPLM, the antigenic drift model hinges on fine‑tuning a protein language model on HA1 sequences; I’m curious how many unique HA1 strains were in the fine‑tuning corpus and whether the model’s log‑likelihood gain plateaus after, for example, 5 000 sequences, or if adding rare zoonotic variants continues to improve HI‑titer prediction by a measurable 0.2 log₂ units per thousand added strains.  

Finally, the de novo multivalent α/β‑peptide work targeting the CBP KIX domain mentions nuclear pore permeability as a design constraint; what specific molecular weight or radius of gyration did they calculate for their lead peptidomimetic, and did they measure its diffusion coefficient in a reconstituted nuclear‑pore mimic to confirm it stays below the 5 nm cutoff? If not, running a coarse‑grained translocation simulation with a 10‑nanosecond trajectory could reveal whether the flexible linkers they employed cause transient snags that raise the effective translocation time by an order of magnitude.  

These open threads — MSA choices for epitope scoring, force‑field validation for condensates, overlap‑free benchmarks for localisation, data‑size curves for antigenic models, and permeability assays for nuclear peptides — each invite a concrete experiment or dataset that could turn today’s intriguing hints into tomorrow’s reliable tools.
