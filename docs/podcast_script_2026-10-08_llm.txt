The biggest revelation today is that AlphaFold 3 doesn't actually read your protein's evolutionary history the way we thought it did. A paper on structural shortcuts in AlphaFold 3 shows that when you strip away the multiple sequence alignment entirely, the model still gets local secondary structure right — alpha helices and beta sheets pop out fine — but the long-range relationships that define the protein's global fold collapse. What's remarkable is that restoring the MSA-enriched pair representation at just forty residues is enough to recover most of that lost organization, even at residue pairs that were never directly touched. And here's the kicker: if you take an alignment built for a completely different protein that happens to share the same fold, but feed it only at structurally corresponding columns, the median TM-score jumps from 0.44 to 0.72. Shift that alignment by just a few residues and it performs worse than having no alignment at all. This tells us that what AlphaFold 3 is really reading from an alignment is a description of the fold itself — a transferable blueprint — not the query's own evolutionary story.

That insight reframes how we think about the model's accuracy and its blind spots. The fold becomes recoverable by roughly block 9 of 48 for most proteins, which is twenty-seven blocks before the decoder can actually render the structure. Without the MSA, that fold never becomes accessible at all. So when AlphaFold 3 nails a structure, it's often because it found a fold-level description in the alignment, not because it inferred everything from first principles.

On the antibody front, OFAntibody is pushing de novo design beyond the simple antigen-antibody handshake into genuinely multi-component territory. Where previous methods like RFantibody were built around a single interface, OFAntibody handles tandem VHH, diabody, and CODV formats with explicit target and epitope conditioning. In nanobody benchmarks it hits a Top-5 enrichment rate of 41.5 percent — that's a 5.39-fold improvement over RFantibody in competitive candidate ranking. For bispecific tasks, hotspot pass rates range from 94 to 100 percent across three evaluated tasks. The energy pass rates vary dramatically by format though: 60 percent for diabody, 13 percent for tandem VHH, and just 8 percent for CODV, which tells us the geometric challenges of multi-interface design are far from solved.

Meanwhile, DyME is stitching together high-throughput mutagenesis with molecular dynamics in a way that static structure-based methods simply can't match. The platform automates everything from identifying interfacial anchor points to running MD simulations on distributed GPU nodes and extracting features like binding free energies and water-site maps. When validated against an experimentally characterized dataset for protein mimicry, DyME's computed binding free energies correlated with experimental Kd values, and its water-site mapping module recovered previously characterized water-mediated interactions. This matters because static methods miss the conformational dynamics that often determine whether a designed binder actually works.

And then there's VAEBAC, which is tackling a problem we've largely ignored: amyloidogenic potential in nucleic-acid-binding proteins. These proteins govern transcription, replication, and genome organization, but we've had no systematic way to ask which ones carry hidden aggregation risk. VAEBAC uses a variational autoencoder framework to predict amyloidogenic propensity across the NABP proteomes of four phylogenetically diverse organisms, and it does so while functionally stratifying those proteins in ways that pure sequence-based methods can't.

Each of these papers shifts the question from "can we predict or design this?" to "what hidden assumptions are we making about how biology actually works?"

What strikes me first about these methods is how each one is really a clever workaround for a problem that would stymie a naive approach. What we're seeing today isn't just better engineering of existing ideas — it's a genuine conceptual shift in how we think about biological information itself. The structural shortcut paper's finding that AlphaFold 3 reads fold-level blueprints from MSAs rather than evolutionary history echoes something we've known since the 1960s but never fully integrated into our computational frameworks: Anfinsen's dogma, that a protein's sequence encodes its structure through physical and thermodynamic principles, not just evolutionary contingency.
