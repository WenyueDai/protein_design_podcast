The biggest shift today is that we're finally cracking open the black box of structure prediction itself, not just treating it as a magic oracle. A paper on de novo binder design using sparse autoencoders inside ESMFold's trunk found that the model's folding computation is driven by structural geometry, not sequence motifs. The authors trained SAEs on intermediate representations and discovered that single representations carry inherited semantics while pair representations compute pairwise geometry — and concept activations from these dictionaries actually forecasted the model's own fold quality. This means we can now validate neural folding circuits against known biochemistry, turning the trunk into something scientifically verifiable rather than a statistical artifact.

That interpretability breakthrough pairs with a second major shift: moving from static structure prediction to dynamic pathway exploration. While AI has revolutionized static protein structure prediction, capturing high-dimensional dynamics remains the frontier. A framework called DA2-GRASP extends pathway exploration beyond miniproteins, suggesting we're finally bridging the gap between predicted structures and the actual folding trajectories that determine function. This matters because misfolding diseases like Alzheimer's and Parkinson's aren't about wrong structures — they're about wrong folding paths.

The third insight flips directed evolution on its head. Instead of starting with massive experimental datasets, a prior-guided adaptive mixture-of-experts framework called JustEvo tackles campaigns that begin with few or no target-specific measurements. This directly addresses the reality that most protein engineering efforts start in data-poor regimes, where heterogeneous protein-model priors need to be strategically deployed rather than naively averaged.

On the therapeutic front, AbOptimizer demonstrates that reinforcement learning can now handle multi-property antibody optimization in a single unified framework. Using PPO training with a transformer-based policy network, it achieved affinity scores up to 0.99 across all starting groups for emibetuzumab, and produced 95% of the top-5 variants across four affinity-specificity starting groups. The key insight is that Pareto-based rewards let you balance competing objectives without predefined weights — something traditional approaches struggle with when optimizing binding affinity, specificity, and humanness simultaneously.

The fifth shift is about grounding predictions in physical reality. GeoMIC, a two-stage framework combining protein language model embeddings with predicted peptide geometry, achieved an AUROC of 0.844 on XUAMP and MIC prediction correlations of 0.794. This shows that incorporating actual geometric constraints — not just sequence patterns — dramatically improves antimicrobial peptide screening, where activity classification and pathogen-specific potency estimation have historically been treated as separate problems.

What ties all five together is a move away from pure sequence-to-function mapping toward mechanisms that respect the physical and geometric realities of protein folding, dynamics, and interaction.
