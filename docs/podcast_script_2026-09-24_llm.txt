The big idea that hit me today is that we might be leaving performance on the table by treating protein language models as pure sequence-prediction machines. ProteinJEPA takes the joint-embedding predictive architecture that worked so well in vision — where LeCun's group showed you don't need to predict pixels, you just need to predict latent representations — and grafts it onto the standard masked language modeling objective we've been using since ESM-1b. The intuition is elegant: when you force a model to reconstruct the exact amino acid at a masked position, you're spending capacity on details that are sometimes genuinely ambiguous, like whether a position wants leucine or isoleucine. By adding a cosine-similarity-based latent prediction loss alongside the MLM objective, ProteinJEPA lets the model learn what matters about a position without overcommitting to a single identity. This echoes what the I-JEPA paper showed for images — that predicting in representation space generalizes better than predicting in input space — and if that transfers to proteins the way the early results suggest, it could change how we pretrain every foundation model we downstream into binder design.

The second shift is more theoretical but equally provocative. For years the protein design community has treated statistical coupling analysis and direct coupling analysis as evidence that structural contacts drive evolutionary covariation — you infer contacts from sequences, and that's why coevolution methods work for structure prediction. The paper on folding stability and observed covariation in the PDB argues that a huge chunk of what we've been attributing to contact constraints is actually selection for folding stability, both against unfolding and against misfolding. They model this with a Potts distribution decomposed into globally shared parameters reflecting mutational bias and site-specific couplings inferred by minimizing KL divergence under a stability constraint. The practical implication is that when you design a sequence and check whether your designed positions are consistent with natural covariation patterns, you might be rejecting perfectly good designs because the couplings you're comparing against encode thermodynamic stability pressures, not just geometric contact — and your design might satisfy stability through a different route.

On the applied side, NRB-FusePS points to where multimodal protein modeling is heading for functional site prediction. They built a framework that combines protein language model embeddings with structural and ligand-binding information to predict BioLiP-defined ligand-binding residues specifically in nuclear receptors. Nuclear receptors are interesting because their ligand-binding domains are drug targets — think estrogen receptor, androgen receptor, PPARs — and knowing which residues line the binding pocket from sequence alone has been unreliable. The multimodal fusion approach matters because it suggests that for functionally important subfamilies, adding ligand-specific modalities on top of PLM embeddings gives you residue-level resolution that pure sequence models miss.

The most clever methodological choice across today's papers is ProteinJEPA's decision to supplement rather than replace masked language modeling. A naive approach would swap MLM entirely for the latent prediction objective, but that would risk losing amino acid identity information — a model that only predicts representations could learn that a position is "hydrophobic" without ever learning whether it's leucine or valine, which matters when you're downstreaming into sequence design. By keeping both objectives, they let the MLM head preserve identity while the JEPA head captures higher-order positional semantics. The thing I'd steal immediately is the cosine similarity loss in representation space — it's cheap, it doesn't require a separate decoder, and it directly addresses the overcommitting problem we've all noticed when ESM models assign near-zero probability to perfectly reasonable substitutions. This shift toward latent prediction in ProteinJEPA isn't just a clever engineering tweak; it's a conceptual pivot that mirrors the broader evolution of representation learning. For years, we've been stuck in the paradigm of generative reconstruction, which is essentially what masked language modeling is. It's the same logic that drove early image models to predict every single pixel of a masked patch. But as we saw with the original I-JEPA work in computer vision, trying to predict every pixel is often a waste of capacity because the high-frequency details are often noise. In proteins, this manifests as the leucine-isoleucine ambiguity. When a model is forced to pick one, it's essentially guessing at a level of granularity that the evolutionary signal doesn't actually support. By moving the objective to a cosine-similarity loss in latent space, we're finally acknowledging that the protein sequence is a lossy compression of the underlying physics. This is a genuine shift from treating the sequence as the ground truth to treating the sequence as a proxy for a latent structural and thermodynamic state.

This connects deeply to the theoretical tension raised by the Potts model paper. For a decade, the field has operated on the assumption that if two residues coevolve, they must be in physical contact. This was the bedrock of the early contact map predictions that paved the way for AlphaFold2. But the idea that selection for folding stability—specifically the pressure to avoid both unfolding and misfolding—can generate these same covariation patterns suggests that our "contact maps" might actually be "stability maps." It reminds me of how NMR in the 1980s shifted our view of proteins from static crystals to dynamic ensembles. We're realizing that the evolutionary signal isn't just a geometric blueprint; it's a record of thermodynamic survival. If the minimum selection principle can recover PDB covariation by minimizing KL divergence under a stability constraint, it means we've been over-interpreting the geometry of evolution.

Looking ahead, I suspect we're moving toward a "physics-integrated" foundation model. The current trend is to train a massive PLM and then fine-tune it for a task, like the multimodal fusion used in NRB-FusePS to identify ligand-binding residues in nuclear receptors. But the real momentum is in baking the physics into the pretraining. I expect to see models that don't just predict the next token or a latent vector, but predict a physical property—like the $\Delta\Delta G$ of a mutation or the quantum-mechanical energy of a peptide backbone—as a primary objective. 

To know if this is real and not just hype, I'll be watching for a model that can predict the conformational ensemble of a 15-residue cyclic peptide with an RMSD under 1.5 Angstroms without using a template. If we can move from predicting a single static structure to predicting a Boltzmann distribution of states directly from a latent-space PLM, we'll have finally bridged the gap between the "language" of proteins and the actual thermodynamics of the folding funnel. That would be the transition from protein linguistics to protein physics.

One heuristic I'm taking away from the ProteinJEPA work is that masked language model logits should be used as a negative filter, never as a positive selector, when you're evaluating designed sequences. What I mean concretely is this: if ESM assigns a probability below one percent to a substitution at a designed position, that's probably a real signal to reject it. But if you're choosing between two candidate designs and one has an MLM log-likelihood of minus two and the other minus four, don't pick the first one just because the model prefers it. The cosine-similarity latent prediction objective in ProteinJEPA exists precisely because the MLM head overcommits to a single amino acid identity at positions where the real evolutionary signal is something looser, like "medium hydrophobic, leucine or isoleucine or valine all work." So the rule is: use MLM probabilities to flag truly implausible residues, but when two candidates are both in the plausible zone, break the tie with a structural or thermodynamic metric, not with the language model score. The failure case is when you're designing for a position that's genuinely specificity-determinant, like an active-site catalytic residue where only one amino acid works. There, the MLM probability is actually telling you something sharp, not overcommitting. The warning sign that you're in that regime is if the position has near-zero entropy in the MSA — if every homolog has the same residue there, trust the MLM.

The Potts model paper gives me a second rule, and it's one that cuts against a habit a lot of us have. The paper shows that a substantial fraction of observed covariation in the PDB can be recovered from a stability-constrained Potts model without any contact information at all — the couplings emerge from selection against both unfolding and misfolding. So a coupling between positions twelve and forty-five might not mean "these are in contact"; it might mean "if you mutate twelve to something destabilizing, you need to compensate at forty-five to maintain the folding free energy." Your design might satisfy stability through a completely different compensation pattern.
