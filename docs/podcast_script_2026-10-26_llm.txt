The field has been treating confidence scores as gospel for years, but a new computational study on IL-23 p19 binders just exposed a fundamental flaw that's been hiding in plain sight. Researchers designing de novo miniprotein binders against the IL-23 cytokine subunit p19 ran the standard RFdiffusion, ProteinMPNN, and ColabFold pipeline across 972 designs, and not a single one produced a reliable predicted interface. The best ipSAE score they could coax out was 0.30, which is essentially noise. But here's the kicker: when they dug into why, they found that single-sequence ColabFold was under-rating binder pLDDT by approximately 56 points, creating a false signal that "binders do not fold." This isn't just a technical glitch; it's a systematic artifact that's likely misled countless design efforts where researchers trusted low pLDDT scores as evidence of failed folding.

The real breakthrough came when they switched to BindCraft, the interface-optimized hallucination method. Suddenly, 42 out of 50 designs cleared an ipSAE threshold of 0.60, with the best hitting 0.804. Five all-alpha-helical leads, ranging from 72 to 104 residues, collectively engaged all six targeted hotspots on the p19 subunit. Two of these leads even docked within the intact p40/p19 heterodimer, and all five showed favorable Rosetta energetics. The p40-alone counter-screen returned ipSAE 0.00 across the board, confirming no spurious interactions. This isn't just about getting better binders; it's about recognizing that the standard confidence metrics we've been using, like ipTM, track construct length rather than interface quality. The ipSAE metric, derived from AlphaFold's predicted aligned error, is length-robust and actually tells you something meaningful about whether your interface prediction is trustworthy.

Meanwhile, in the world of agentic drug design, a completely different kind of confidence problem is emerging. A team analyzing a full log of an AI agent optimizing ligands in protein pockets through iterative docking and editing discovered something counterintuitive about what knowledge actually transfers across targets. They built an experience base from probe edits and tested whether keys, combinations of site contacts, action type, and round number, could separate improving from worsening edits on unseen pockets. Using contacts at the edited site, the action, and the round as a key sharply separated improving from worsening edits, but this separation was largely explained by how much the edit changed the molecule's size, the action, and the round, none of which describes the target. Naming the target outright separated improving from worsening edits several times more sharply than the transferable keys, but for a pocket absent from the base, exact-match target descriptions rarely recurred, while coarser descriptions provided little separation. The signals that did transfer primarily described the edit and the molecule it produced rather than the target itself.

This reframes how we should think about experience bases in molecular optimization. Rather than building databases of target-specific knowledge that we hope will generalize, we should be capturing the edit-molecule relationships that actually carry across protein pockets. The experience base may be more useful for prioritizing an agent's own proposals for docking than for directly prescribing molecular edits in new pockets. It's a humbling reminder that in protein design, as in so many fields, the most transferable knowledge is often the most general.

The deeper story here isn't about IL-23 or drug design agents at all — it's about how we've been treating protein confidence as a solved problem when it's still fundamentally a question of statistical mechanics meeting information theory. The ipSAE versus ipTM distinction echoes what the AlphaFold2 paper showed about contact maps: that raw prediction accuracy and interface reliability are decoupled, because the former tracks evolutionary co-variation while the latter depends on the physical chemistry of the binding interface. This goes back further, to the classic result that protein folding is a free energy landscape problem, not just a sequence-to-structure mapping. When single-sequence ColabFold under-rates pLDDT by 56 points, it's not a bug in the neural network — it's the model's implicit prior about co-evolutionary signal being violated by a de novo sequence that has no evolutionary history to lean on. The buried-surface-area check works precisely because it bypasses the evolutionary inference entirely and asks a purely geometric question: does this interface have enough physical contact area to be stable?

This is a genuine conceptual shift, not incremental engineering. For decades, computational protein design operated on the assumption that if you could predict the structure, you could predict the function. The field built its confidence hierarchies around pLDDT and ipTM because those were the metrics AlphaFold delivered, and AlphaFold was the best game in town. But the IL-23 p19 work shows that for de novo binders — sequences with no homologous templates — those metrics are systematically misleading. The 56-point gap isn't noise; it's a structural artifact of trying to apply a model trained on natural protein families to synthetic sequences that violate every assumption about co-evolutionary coupling. This is similar to how NMR revealed protein dynamics in the 1980s: the static crystal structure was never the whole story, and the field had to expand its definition of what constituted a reliable prediction.

Looking forward, the real momentum is in closing the loop between computational confidence and physical reality. The agentic drug design paper's experience base — indexed by edit-action and molecular-size-change rather than target identity — points toward a future where design agents carry forward only the transferable physics of molecular editing, not the idiosyncrasies of specific protein pockets. The evidence that contacts, action type, and round number separate improving from worsening edits better than target descriptions suggests we're moving toward a regime where the agent's internal model of "what makes a good edit" is grounded in chemical intuition rather than target-specific memorization.
