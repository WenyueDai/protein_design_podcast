The CAPRI community just handed us the clearest picture yet of what the AlphaFold era actually changed for protein-protein docking — and what it didn't. The 8th CAPRI evaluation, covering rounds 47 through 55, reads like a before-and-after study written in real time. Before AlphaFold, the bottleneck was getting a decent monomer structure to dock in the first place. Now the monomer problem is largely solved, and the field has shifted to a different question: given a near-perfect chain, how do you find the right partner orientation, and how do you score it?

The evaluation paper makes this divide explicit. Rounds 47 to 55 span the transition, and the difficulty didn't drop — if anything, the targets got harder, with more domain-domain, protein-peptide, and protein-DNA cases, plus homo- and hetero-meric interfaces that AlphaFold alone struggles to resolve. The HDOCK team showed what happens when you feed AlphaFold models into a traditional docking pipeline: their AlphaFold-enhanced version competed across all eleven targets in the 8th edition, but the gains weren't uniform. For some targets the AlphaFold starting structures were so good that docking became a refinement problem; for others, the conformational flexibility at the interface still demanded exhaustive sampling.

pyDock took a complementary angle — they kept their energy-based scoring function but applied it to both traditional docking decoys and AlphaFold-generated multimers. The insight there is subtle: physics-based scoring still matters, maybe more than ever, because AlphaFold's confidence metrics don't always correlate with interface accuracy. A model can have high pLDDT across the board and still place a side chain wrong at the binding hotspot. pyDock's electrostatics and desolvation terms catch some of those errors that pure deep learning misses.

When we look at the transition captured in the 8th CAPRI evaluation, we aren't just seeing a change in software; we are witnessing a fundamental shift in the underlying philosophy of structural biology. For decades, the field was anchored in the physics of the search space. We treated docking as a massive sampling problem governed by the laws of thermodynamics—trying to find the global free energy minimum in a landscape of electrostatic attractions and desolvation penalties. This was the era of the rigid-body docking and the exhaustive search for the lowest energy decoy. It echoes the classic era of NMR and X-ray crystallography, where the challenge was extracting signal from noise and understanding how a single protein folded into its native state.

The AlphaFold revolution, as highlighted by the transition from the pre- to post-AlphaFold era in these rounds, represents a move from physics-first to information-first modeling. Instead of asking, "What is the most energetically favorable way these two surfaces can touch?" we are now asking, "What does the evolutionary co-variation in these sequences imply about their proximity?" This is a conceptual leap from statistical mechanics to information theory. We are essentially using the massive, implicit dataset of all known protein evolution to bypass the sampling problem. But as the pyDock results remind us, this leap isn't a total replacement. When AlphaFold places a side chain incorrectly at a binding hotspot despite high pLDDT, it’s a signal that the information-first approach is still missing the fine-grained, local physics that governs the actual chemistry of the interface.

This brings us to the real frontier. We are moving away from the era of "structure prediction" and into the era of "interface refinement and design." The fact that the 8th CAPRI targets included more complex protein-peptide and protein-DNA interactions suggests that the "solved" monomer problem is just the baseline. The real momentum isn't in predicting a static quaternary structure, but in modeling the ensemble of states that exist during a binding event. 

If we want to know if this shift is real or just hype, we need to look for a specific kind of evidence over the next two to three years: the emergence of "physics-aware" generative models. I'm not talking about just more AlphaFold-style transformers, but models that can explicitly incorporate the desolvation and electrostatic constraints that the pyDock team found so useful. We should watch for a convergence where the high-level topological accuracy of deep learning is seamlessly integrated with the local, high-resolution accuracy of physics-based scoring. If we see a new generation of tools that can predict not just a single docking pose, but a statistically accurate Boltzmann distribution of interface conformations—especially for those difficult protein-peptide targets—then we'll know we've truly moved past the AlphaFold divide and into a new era of true biomolecular simulation.

If you are moving from binder discovery to high-precision interface engineering, you need to adopt a "hybrid validation" heuristic: never trust a high pLDDT or high confidence score as a proxy for binding pose accuracy in complex interfaces. The CAPRI 8th evaluation results, specifically the pyDock findings, provide a clear rule for your design pipeline. If you have a computationally designed binder where the AlphaFold-Multimer confidence is high, but your physics-based scoring—specifically terms for electrostatics and desolvation—is flagging a high-energy penalty, you must treat the model as a failure. In the pyDock study, the energy-based scoring was able to differentiate between correct and incorrect poses that deep learning metrics alone missed. For a designer, this means your "go/no-go" decision for a synthesis batch shouldn't just be a DockQ score or a pLDDT threshold; it should be a consensus between the learned topology and the physical feasibility of the side-chain packing.

A second heuristic is to treat protein-peptide and protein-DNA targets as a separate class of risk in your design workflows. The 8th CAPRI evaluation showed that the difficulty spiked significantly for these specific interaction types compared to domain-domain interfaces. If you are designing a peptide mimetic or a DNA-binding protein, do not assume that the success you see in domain-domain docking will translate. The rule here is: if the target involves a highly flexible peptide or a charged DNA backbone, you must increase your sampling depth by at least an order of magnitude. The modeling of Round 55 targets suggests that the "AlphaFold-enhanced" approach—using AlphaFold to provide the starting geometry and then running traditional docking for refinement—is the only way to handle the conformational flexibility inherent in these targets. If you skip the docking refinement step for a peptide target, you are likely just accepting a high-confidence hallucination of a binding pose.

Finally, use physics-based scoring as a negative filter rather than a primary optimizer. If a design shows a massive desolvation penalty or an impossible electrostatic clash, kill it immediately, regardless of how well the AlphaFold-Multimer model "looks." This rule breaks down, however, when you are working with highly intrinsically disordered proteins or extremely transient interfaces where the "correct" pose is actually a high-energy state in a larger ensemble. If you see your physics scores consistently rejecting every single design candidate in a specific family, that is your warning sign: you aren't failing at design, you are likely using a rigid-body scoring assumption on a target that requires an ensemble-based approach.
