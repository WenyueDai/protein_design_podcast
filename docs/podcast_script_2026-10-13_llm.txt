The biggest shift today is that AI is no longer just predicting static structures — it's starting to predict the messy, dynamic reality of how proteins actually behave in disease and in drug discovery. For years, the field operated on the assumption that if you could just nail the folded structure, you'd cracked the code. AlphaFold 2 taught us that was wrong, and AlphaFold 3 is pushing that lesson even further by modeling not just proteins but their interactions with ligands, DNA, RNA, and other molecules all at once. A paper on drug innovation in the AI era argues this multi-molecule co-folding capability is what finally lets us move beyond the old, linear drug discovery pipeline where you screen compounds against a single static target. Instead, you can now ask: what does this small molecule actually do when it bumps into a protein that's also bound to RNA, or another protein partner? That's a fundamental reframe of how we think about target engagement.

But structure alone still misses the real action in many diseases. Take amyloid fibrils — those tangled protein aggregates behind Alzheimer's and Parkinson's. For decades, predictors tried to flag amyloid-forming regions based on sequence patterns alone, but they consistently missed residues that show up in actual disease fibril structures. AmyloCore-ML flips this by training directly on experimentally resolved fibril cores from the Amyloid Atlas database, using protein language model embeddings from ESM-2 and ProtT5 to encode each residue. The result is a model that hits an AUROC of 0.88 and AUPRC of 0.85, and when they ran locked full-protein scans, the ESM-2/ExtraTrees model localized experimental cores with a mean peak distance of just 12.4 residues. That's the difference between flagging a vague stretch of 50 amino acids and pinpointing the exact 15-residue segment that becomes structured in the fibril. For someone designing aggregation inhibitors, that precision is the difference between a drug that works and one that doesn't.

Meanwhile, the field's obsession with retrospective benchmarks is finally getting called out. A large-scale prospective evaluation across hundreds of Mac1-ligand complexes — that's the main protease from SARS-CoV-2 — tested co-folding methods on three separate virtual screens, not just cherry-picked test sets. This matters because for years, every paper claimed their co-folding method worked brilliantly, but those claims were based on training data leakage and tiny test sets. When you actually run the method prospectively on real screening campaigns, the hit rates and affinity rankings tell a very different story. The paper doesn't spell out the exact numbers in the abstract, but the fact that they're testing across hundreds of complexes and three independent screens is itself the insight: we're finally moving toward evaluation that mirrors real drug discovery workflows.

And then there's the quiet revolution happening in how we think about protein dynamics. A paper on proline isotope labeling for NMR spectroscopy is solving a decades-old problem: proline residues are everywhere in disordered proteins and enzyme hinges, but they lack amide protons, making them invisible to conventional NMR detection. The chemoenzymatic labeling approach lets researchers finally track proline dynamics in solution and under magic-angle spinning conditions. This isn't just a technical fix — it's a reminder that allostery and conformational change, not just static binding pockets, are where the real regulatory logic lives. Which brings us to a paper showing that sequence-encoded dynamics can diversify allostery within a single conserved protein scaffold. The implication is profound: you don't need entirely new folds to get new regulatory behaviors. Subtle sequence changes tune the dynamic ensemble, and that's something structure prediction alone would never reveal.

All of this points to one overarching shift: the field is moving from static structure to dynamic function, from retrospective benchmarks to prospective validation, and from single-molecule thinking to multi-component reality.

The deeper story here isn't about any single method — it's about a century-old tension in biology between structure and dynamics, between the static snapshot and the ensemble of conformations that a protein actually samples. This tension has shaped the field from the very beginning. When Perutz and Kendrew solved the first globular protein structures in the 1950s, they already knew that hemoglobin's function depended on conformational change — the T-to-R transition that allows oxygen binding and release. But the tools to study that transition were limited to crude spectroscopic methods and low-resolution electron density maps. Fast-forward to the 1980s, when NMR began revealing that even "simple" globular proteins like ubiquitin weren't static at all — they existed as dynamic ensembles, with side chains flipping between rotameric states on picosecond-to-nanosecond timescales. The proline labeling paper today is a direct descendant of that lineage, solving a problem that has plagued NMR spectroscopists since the technique's early days: proline's lack of an amide proton makes it invisible to conventional detection schemes, yet proline is disproportionately represented in disordered regions and hinge loops where dynamics matter most.

This echoes what the AlphaFold2 paper showed about contact maps — that evolutionary couplings, extracted from multiple sequence alignments, encode not just the final folded state but the statistical preferences of residue pairs across the entire ensemble of sequences that nature has sampled. AlphaFold3 extends that logic to multi-molecule complexes, but the fundamental insight remains: the evolutionary record is a proxy for the energy landscape, and deep learning models are learning to read that landscape directly from sequence. The Mac1-ligand co-folding work sits squarely in this tradition, but with a crucial twist — instead of asking whether a model can recapitulate a single static structure, it asks whether the model can predict the right conformational state when a ligand binds. That's the real test of whether these methods capture dynamics or just memorize static snapshots.

Where this is heading becomes clearer when you look at the convergence across these papers. AmyloCore-ML trains on experimentally resolved fibril cores, not on sequence-based heuristics — it's learning the structural signature of incorporation directly from the data. The proline labeling work provides the experimental handle to validate dynamic predictions. The Mac1 evaluation framework demands prospective testing on real screening campaigns. And the allostery paper shows that sequence-encoded dynamics can diversify function within a single scaffold. Together, these point toward a future where protein design doesn't just optimize for a single static structure but engineers the entire conformational ensemble — where you can dial in the right balance of rigidity and flexibility, the right pattern of correlated motions, the right energetic landscape for function.

The evidence that this direction is real, not hype, will come in the next two to three years from three sources. First, we'll see whether co-folding models can consistently predict ligand-induced conformational changes across diverse protein families — not just on retrospective benchmarks but on prospective screens where the answer isn't known in advance. Second, we'll see whether dynamics-aware design tools can produce proteins with engineered allosteric properties — proteins that change conformation in response to a designed trigger, with measurable changes in dynamics that correlate with function. Third, we'll see whether the integration of experimental validation methods like proline labeling into the design cycle becomes routine, not exceptional. If any of these fail to materialize, the field will retreat back to static structure optimization. But if they succeed, we're looking at a fundamental shift in how we think about protein function — from a single structure to a dynamic ensemble, from a static target to a conformational landscape that can be engineered.
