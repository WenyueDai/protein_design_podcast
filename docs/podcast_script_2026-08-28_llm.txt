The papers together paint a picture of rapid progress, but several tensions emerge when we look closely at the assumptions behind the celebrated results. The geometric‑annotation approach in the ESM‑2 sparse autoencoder study leans heavily on the idea that local Cα backbone patterns can be read off as meaningful features in a language model’s latent space. Yet the authors only show that removing these features shifts predicted contact maps; they do not demonstrate that the shift corresponds to a loss of biological function or that the features are causal rather than merely correlative. Without testing the ablation on a diverse set of proteins or measuring downstream effects like binding affinity or stability, the claim that these features “link mechanistic interpretability to structural biology” rests on a narrow validation that could be explained by the model simply learning to reproduce statistical regularities in the training data.

The GPCR pipeline paper offers a compelling workflow, but its success hinges on a stringently chosen set of computational filters—DSSP‑defined helices, pLDDT above 80, helix‑phase deviation under 50°, and a membrane‑distance cutoff of five angstroms. For the vasopressin receptor, only five of sixty‑five DSSP‑filtered constructs cleared all four hurdles, and the authors acknowledge that fifty‑three of two hundred eighty‑seven receptors yielded zero candidates at all. This suggests the pipeline may be discarding viable constructs that fall just outside the arbitrary thresholds, and the reliance on ColabFold models for filter evaluation introduces another layer of uncertainty, since those predictions have known failure modes for membrane proteins. The most non‑obvious insight is that a language model’s internal geometry can be read directly from the protein backbone, letting us steer predictions by editing abstract features rather than residues.  
The most elegant idea is the NOAH‑ARK1 concept of using a computationally vetted BRIL insertion site as a universal “structural handle” that turns any GPCR into a cryo‑EM‑ready target with just one construct test.

Thinking about the geometric annotation work on ESM‑2, I wonder how the FDR‑controlled discovery across those eight million layers would change if we swapped the Cα backbone for side‑chain centroids or for hydrogen‑bond networks — would we still see the same residue‑level patterns that distinguished features with identical database labels, or would new functional motifs emerge? The paper showed that ablating those geometric features shifted contact‑prediction outputs, but it didn’t quantify how much the shift varied across protein families; tracking the magnitude of that shift as a function of family size or evolutionary depth could reveal whether geometric interpretability is more universal for small, fast‑folding domains than for large multidomain assemblies.

The GPCR pipeline’s reliance on four filters — DSSP helix continuity, pLDDT > 80 at the junction, helix phase Δθ < 50°, and membrane distance > 5 Å — produced five viable V2R constructs out of sixty‑five DSSP‑filtered candidates. I’m curious about the failure modes of the remaining sixty constructs: did they fail because of steric clashes predicted by ColabFold, because the fusion disrupted lipid‑annulus packing, or because the BRIL tag itself aggregated? Running a systematic mutagenesis scan on the linker length between BRIL and the transmembrane helices, say from three to fifteen residues, and measuring FSEC monodispersity could map a tolerance landscape that would help generalize the approach to other class A receptors where TM5‑TM6 is less amenable.

RegimeFormer’s residue‑level summaries covered 407 million residues from a million‑protein subset, yet the paper notes that fifty‑three of the 287 receptors screened in the GPCR study yielded zero viable candidates. Could the perturbation regimes identified by RegimeFormer predict which receptors are intrinsically refractory to BRIL insertion? One experiment would be to compute regime scores for each GPCR’s TM5‑TM6 region and test whether low regime scores correlate with the absence of filters‑passing constructs. If so, regime‑based priors could become a pre‑screen for construct design pipelines.

Finally, the energy comparison of spiking versus artificial networks on microcontrollers — though we lack the raw numbers here — raises the question of how measurement granularity affects fairness. Did the authors account for leakage current during idle periods, or for the cost of converting analog sensor inputs to spike trains? Replicating the benchmark on a RISC‑V core with cycle‑accurate power profiling and reporting energy per inference event, not just per second, would expose whether SNNs retain their advantage when real‑world sensor noise and communication overhead are folded in.
