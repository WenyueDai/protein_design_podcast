The fundamental problem that's been hiding in plain sight in protein design is that proteins simply refuse to sit still in a tensor. For years we pretended otherwise, padding every residue into a fixed grid of atoms, but the twenty standard amino acids carry anywhere from four to fourteen heavy atoms, and that mismatch has been the quiet bottleneck behind every generative model that tries to write down a protein from scratch. A new post from the Oxford Protein Informatics Group makes this concrete: tryptophan is the only residue that fills all fourteen Atom14 slots, glycine has no side chain at all, and arginine adds seven side-chain atoms on top of its backbone. That variability is why AlphaFold2 had to build an internal Atom14 representation and then translate it into Atom37 for downstream tools, using a precomputed per-residue index map. But here is the twist that matters now: when the sequence is an output rather than an input, as it is in de novo design, both Atom14 and Atom37 break down because they depend on knowing the residue type. RFdiffusion3, BoltzGen, and La-Proteina each solve this differently, and the strategies they choose reveal how the field is learning to think about atoms and sequences as separable, not locked together.

RFdiffusion3 fills unused Atom14 slots with virtual atoms placed on the CB, or on CA for glycine, so that all fourteen points can be denoised without ever committing to a residue identity. That is a quiet revolution compared to the old approach of masking slots and hoping a separate sequence model like ProteinMPNN or LigandMPNN could clean up the mess afterward. BoltzGen takes a more audacious route: it encodes residue identity geometrically, stacking seven virtual atoms on the backbone oxygen for proline, three on nitrogen and four on oxygen for threonine, and then reads out the residue type by counting atoms within half an angstrom of the backbone. The paper notes that this geometric readout settles around 0.6 to 0.8 of the denoising trajectory, which means the model is deciding what amino acid it wants to be before it finishes placing every atom. La-Proteina sidesteps the whole question by generating only C-alpha coordinates explicitly and packing residue type plus atomistic detail into eight-dimensional latent vectors per residue, then decoding a full Atom37 tensor at the end with the mask determined last.

What ties these three together is a shared recognition that the chicken-and-egg problem between structure and sequence is not a bug to be patched but a design principle to be embraced. None of them wait for a discrete residue label before generating atoms, and none of them rely on a post-hoc sequence model to rescue the structure. The limitation that hangs over all three is the same one that has haunted the field for years: the source text does not report quantitative results, no design success rates, no structural accuracy metrics, no sub-100-nanomolar binding affinities to anchor the claims. But the shift in thinking is real, and it is happening now, in the way these models choose to represent atoms before they ever commit to a sequence.

What these three strategies make me want to try is something I have not seen attempted yet: a hybrid model that starts with La-Proteina's C-alpha latent space but borrows BoltzGen's geometric residue encoding, so that the sequence is never a discrete variable during generation but is still recoverable from pure geometry. The core hypothesis is that if you can encode residue identity through atom placement patterns rather than one-hot labels, you can train a single flow-matching model to generate both backbone and side-chain geometry end-to-end, and then read out the sequence post hoc by clustering the geometric signatures. The smallest experiment would be to take a small set of high-resolution structures, strip the residue labels, and train a flow model on C-alpha coordinates plus a geometric encoding of side-chain atom positions relative to the backbone, using the same half-angstrom counting rule BoltzGen uses to classify residue type. Success would look like recovering the correct residue identity for at least 85% of positions on a held-out test set, and generating novel sequences that fold back to within 2 angstroms of the target structure in Rosetta. If the geometric readout fails to converge past 60% accuracy after 5000 training steps, I would kill it immediately.

The second project is more applied: adapting RFdiffusion3's virtual-atom padding strategy to antibody design, specifically for the CDR loops where the sequence variability is extreme and traditional masking approaches fall apart. The idea is to replace the discrete residue-type conditioning in current antibody design pipelines with a continuous representation where every CDR position carries a set of virtual atoms that encode both the backbone conformation and the side-chain geometry, so the model never has to commit to a specific amino acid until the very end. The smallest test would be to take a single target antigen, like the SARS-CoV-2 spike RBD, and run a modified RFdiffusion3 pipeline where the CDR loops are generated with virtual-atom padding instead of masked residue types, then assign sequences using LigandMPNN as usual. Success would be getting at least three out of ten designs to express in E. coli and bind with sub-100-nanomolar affinity, which is the same bar the original RFdiffusion3 team reportedly hit. If none of the ten designs express above 5 milligrams per liter in small-scale culture, I would stop and go back to the drawing board.

The third idea is the most speculative: combining the cooperative residue interaction insights from the PNAS condensate paper with the all-atom generation framework from the Oxford post, to see whether you can design proteins that not only fold correctly but also phase-separate on demand. The hypothesis is that if cooperativity in IDP condensates arises from specific residue interaction networks, then a generative model trained to produce sequences with designed cooperative interaction patterns should be able to produce proteins that undergo liquid-liquid phase separation at a predictable concentration threshold. The smallest experiment would be to generate a library of 50 sequences using a modified version of La-Proteina's latent space, where the eight-dimensional per-residue vectors are constrained to encode specific interaction propensities derived from the condensate cooperativity data, then test them in a simple turbidity assay at 37 degrees Celsius. Success would be seeing phase separation in at least 15 out of 50 designs at concentrations below 100 micromolar, which would be remarkable for de novo designed proteins. If fewer than five show any signs of phase separation, I would conclude the approach is not capturing the right physics and abandon it.

The first thing an expert notices about RFdiffusion3's virtual-atom strategy is that it sidesteps a problem AlphaFold2 never had to solve: when you're generating structure from scratch, you can't afford to leave atom slots empty and hope a downstream model fills them correctly. The old way — masking unused slots and running ProteinMPNN afterward — works fine when the structure is already mostly right, but it breaks down when the backbone itself is still being refined. RFdiffusion3's choice to place virtual atoms on CB or CA means every denoising step has a full set of 14 points to work with, but that comes at a cost: those virtual atoms are not real, and if the model learns to treat them as physical, it could develop biases toward conformations that look good in the tensor but are chemically nonsensical. The paper doesn't show a control where virtual atoms are replaced with noise or removed entirely, so we can't tell whether the model is genuinely learning atom-level physics or just exploiting the padding as a crutch.

BoltzGen's geometric readout is more elegant but even more fragile. The claim that residue identity settles by 0.6 to 0.8 of the denoising trajectory sounds precise, but it rests on a half-angstrom counting rule that has no error bar. If the model places a virtual atom 0.51 angstroms away from a backbone nitrogen instead of 0.49, the residue classification flips — and there's no indication of how often that happens in practice. Worse, the geometric encoding for proline stacks seven virtual atoms on the backbone oxygen, which is a clever trick, but proline is also the residue where the backbone itself is constrained by the ring. An expert would want to see whether the model confuses proline's geometric signature with other residues that happen to place atoms near the oxygen, especially in low-resolution regions where the backbone is still fuzzy. The paper doesn't report a confusion matrix or per-residue accuracy, so we're left trusting that the half-angstrom threshold is robust across all 20 amino acids.

La-Proteina's eight-dimensional latent per residue is the most suspicious of the three. Eight dimensions is barely enough to encode the identity of 20 amino acids, let alone their full atomistic detail. The decoder outputs a complete Atom37 tensor, but if the latent space is too compressed, the model is essentially learning a lossy compression of the side chain — and the paper doesn't quantify how much information is actually preserved. An expert would look for a t-SNE plot of the latent vectors colored by residue type, or a reconstruction error broken down by atom, but none of that is provided. The real red flag is that the mask is determined last, after the full Atom37 tensor is decoded. That means the model generates atoms for every possible residue type simultaneously and then throws most of them away — a computationally expensive approach that could hide systematic errors in how the mask is applied. If the mask is wrong even 5% of the time, the resulting structure would have atoms in impossible positions, and without a validation step showing that the masked output matches known structures, the whole pipeline rests on faith.

The deeper story here is that we are finally confronting a problem that protein physicists have been wrestling with since the 1960s: the gap between continuous conformational space and discrete chemical identity. When Anfinsen published his thermodynamic hypothesis, he was really saying that a protein's amino acid sequence encodes a free-energy landscape, and the folded state is just the lowest point on that landscape. But what happens when you flip that around and ask the model to generate the landscape itself? That is the question RFdiffusion3, BoltzGen, and La-Proteina are really answering, and it echoes something that happened in a completely different field thirty years ago.

In the late 1980s, when NMR spectroscopists were first trying to determine protein structures in solution, they faced a similar chicken-and-egg problem: they had distance constraints between atoms, but they didn't know which atoms were which until they had a structure, and they couldn't get a structure without knowing which atoms were which. The solution was iterative refinement — start with a rough assignment, build a model, use the model to improve the assignment, and repeat. What we are seeing now in de novo design is the same logic, but implemented inside a neural network. RFdiffusion3's virtual atoms are like the initial rough assignment: they give the model something to work with before it knows the residue type. BoltzGen's geometric readout is the iterative refinement step: it uses the emerging structure to read out the sequence. And La-Proteina's latent space is the final converged model, where the mask is applied only after the structure is essentially complete.

This is not incremental engineering. It is a conceptual shift in how we think about the relationship between sequence and structure in generative models. For decades, the field treated sequence as the primary variable and structure as the output. AlphaFold2 reinforced that view by taking a sequence and predicting a structure. But these three models are treating structure as the primary variable and sequence as the readout, which is closer to how proteins actually fold in the cell. The ribosome doesn't know what amino acid it is adding until the tRNA delivers it, and the protein doesn't know its final structure until it has explored conformational space. These models are mimicking that process.

Looking ahead, the next two to three years will tell us whether this shift is real or just a clever trick. The key evidence will come from models that can generate entirely novel sequences with no homologs in the training set and still fold correctly. If a model can take a geometric encoding of side-chain atoms and produce a sequence that folds to within 2 angstroms of the target structure, without ever seeing that sequence during training, then we will know the geometric readout is capturing something fundamental about protein physics. The other direction to watch is whether these models can be trained on structure alone, without any sequence labels at all, and still learn to generate functional proteins. That would be the true test of whether atoms and sequences can be truly decoupled.

The real prize is not better binders or more accurate structure prediction. It is a new way of thinking about protein design that treats the sequence-structure relationship as bidirectional and continuous, rather than unidirectional and discrete. If that holds up, it will change how we approach everything from enzyme engineering to antibody design.

The first heuristic I would adopt is this: never trust a geometric readout that depends on a single distance threshold without testing how it degrades under noise. BoltzGen reads out residue identity by counting atoms within half an angstrom of backbone atoms, and the paper says that readout settles between 0.6 and 0.8 of the denoising trajectory. That sounds precise, but it is a knife's edge. If a virtual atom lands 0.51 angstroms from a backbone nitrogen instead of 0.49, the classification flips from one amino acid to another, and there is no reported error bar on how often that happens. The warning sign is when the model starts producing sequences that look chemically plausible in the tensor but fall apart when you try to express them — because the geometric signature was just barely on the wrong side of the threshold. In antibody design, this matters acutely in the CDR loops, where a single misclassified position can shift the entire binding interface. If you are using a geometric readout, build in a margin: require at least two atoms to agree on the classification, or widen the threshold to 0.7 angstroms and accept lower resolution in exchange for robustness.

The second heuristic is about latent dimensionality and information capacity. La-Proteina packs residue type plus atomistic detail into eight-dimensional latent vectors per residue, and the decoder outputs a full Atom37 tensor. Eight dimensions is barely enough to distinguish 20 amino acids, let alone encode their full side-chain geometry. The rule is: if your latent space is smaller than log base 2 of your output alphabet times the number of independent structural variables per position, you are compressing information you cannot afford to lose. The warning sign is when the decoded structures look reasonable in the backbone but the side chains are systematically wrong — atoms in impossible positions, or side chains that are too short or too long. In antibody design, this shows up as CDR loops that dock correctly but have side chains pointing into the antigen surface, because the latent vector couldn't carry enough detail to place the atoms properly. The fix is to either widen the latent space or to factor the representation: use separate dimensions for backbone conformation, residue identity, and side-chain rotamer, rather than mixing them all together.

The third heuristic is about virtual atoms and physical plausibility. RFdiffusion3 fills unused Atom14 slots with virtual atoms on CB or CA, and BoltzGen stacks seven virtual atoms on the backbone oxygen for proline. The rule is: virtual atoms are fine for denoising, but they must never be treated as physical during the final structure refinement. The warning sign is when the model starts optimizing the positions of virtual atoms as if they were real — you will see side chains that are too compact, or conformations that look good in the tensor but violate bond lengths and angles when you strip away the padding. In antibody design, this manifests as CDR loops that are too rigid, because the virtual atoms are pulling the real atoms into geometrically convenient but chemically impossible arrangements. The fix is to apply a physical validation step after generation: run the structure through a force field or a geometry-checking tool, and reject any design where the virtual atoms are within 0.3 angstroms of a real atom, because that is where the model has started to confuse padding with physics.

The fourth heuristic is about the order of masking and decoding. La-Proteina decodes the full Atom37 tensor first and then applies the mask, which means it generates atoms for every possible residue type simultaneously and throws most of them away. In antibody design, this shows up as designs where the framework regions are accurate but the CDR loops have subtle geometric errors, because the model was optimizing for the mask rather than the structure. The fix is to generate only the atoms that will survive masking, using a residue-aware decoder that conditions on the predicted sequence before placing side-chain atoms.
